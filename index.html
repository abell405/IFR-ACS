IR.I.A.K1

Certification requirements, recent flight experience, and recordkeeping

61.65 Instrument rating eligibility

  • Hold at least a private pilot certificate with the appropriate category rating
  • Read, speak, write, and understand English
  • Ground training or home study endorsement, then pass the knowledge test (IRA)
  • Flight training endorsement for the practical test

61.65(d) Aeronautical experience — airplane

  • 50 hours cross-country PIC, of which at least 10 in airplanes
  • 40 hours actual or simulated instrument, of which 15 with a CFII, including:
    • 3 hours of instrument training in the 2 calendar months before the practical test
    • An IFR cross-country: 250 NM along airways or ATC-directed routing, on a filed IFR flight plan, an approach at each airport, and 3 different kinds of approaches using navigation systems

No more than 20 hours total from devices, except the 30-hour Part 142 case. An authorized instructor must be present and sign for training time.

61.57(c) Instrument currency — 6 HITS

Within the 6 calendar months before the month of the flight, performed and logged in actual or simulated conditions (aircraft, FFS, FTD, or ATD):

  • 6 instrument approaches
  • Holding procedures and tasks
  • Intercepting and Tracking courses through the use of navigational electronic Systems

A device used for currency must represent the category of aircraft. No instructor is required for currency in a device.

Recency timeline

Months 0–6: 6 HITS logged → current
Months 7–12: grace period. Not current for IFR. Regain with 6 HITS using a safety pilot or a device
After 12 months: IPC required (61.57(d))

Safety pilot (91.109(b))

  • At least a private pilot certificate with category and class ratings for the aircraft
  • Current medical (or BasicMed), since the safety pilot is a required crewmember
  • Adequate vision forward and to each side, or a competent observer who fills in
  • The airplane needs fully functioning dual controls

Recordkeeping — 61.51(g)

  • Log instrument time only when flying solely by reference to instruments in actual or simulated conditions
  • For each approach used for currency: location and type of approach, and the name of the safety pilot if one was required
  • Device time: the training device, time, and content

When does an approach count? (FAA legal interpretations)

  • Fly the final approach segment by instruments down to MDA or DA, established on each required segment (or vectored to final)
  • In simulated IMC, stay under the view-limiting device to minimums
  • If you break out in actual IMC during the final segment, the approach still counts

Other currency that still applies

  • 61.56 Flight review every 24 calendar months to act as PIC: at least 1 hour of ground and 1 hour of flight training with a CFI. A practical test for a new certificate or rating counts, so passing the instrument checkride resets the flight review. A phase of the FAA WINGS program also counts. A CFI renewing their certificate skips the ground hour
  • 61.57(a) To carry passengers: 3 takeoffs and landings in the preceding 90 days in the same category, class, and type (if a type rating is required). Full stop in a tailwheel
  • 61.57(b) Passengers at night: 3 takeoffs and landings to a full stop during the period from 1 hour after sunset to 1 hour before sunrise, in the preceding 90 days

Documents — 61.3

  • In your physical possession or readily accessible: pilot certificate, medical certificate (or, under BasicMed, a valid US driver's license), and a government-issued photo ID

Endorsements and type ratings — 61.31

Instrument students often move into faster airplanes for the long IFR cross-country. Know which logbook endorsements you need first:

  • Complex (61.31(e)): flaps, retractable gear, and a controllable-pitch propeller (or FADEC)
  • High performance (61.31(f)): an engine of more than 200 hp
  • High altitude (61.31(g)): pressurized, with a service ceiling or max operating altitude above 25,000 ft MSL
  • Tailwheel (61.31(i)). Type rating (61.31(a)): large airplanes over 12,500 lb, turbojets, and others the FAA specifies
IR.I.A.K2

Privileges and limitations

An instrument rating is required to:

  • Act as PIC under IFR, or in weather below VFR minimums (61.3(e))
  • Fly in Class A airspace (91.135)
  • Fly Special VFR at night, sunset to sunrise (91.157); the airplane must also be IFR-equipped
  • As a commercial pilot, carry passengers for hire on cross-country flights over 50 NM or at night (61.133)

Limitations

  • The rating is category-specific: Instrument – Airplane covers single and multiengine airplanes you hold class ratings for
  • Legal to fly IFR does not mean legal for the airplane: IFR equipment (91.205(d)) and inspections (91.411, 91.413) also apply
  • No Part 91 takeoff minimums, but your own minimums and 91.13 (careless or reckless) still apply

The instrument rating adds no pay privileges

On a private certificate you're still bound by 61.113. Flying a friend somewhere in IMC doesn't change the compensation rules.

61.113 private pilot privileges — PPSCRIPT

  • PIC and carry passengers, not for compensation or hire
  • Pro rata: pay at least your proportional share of the operating expenses (fuel, oil, airport fees, rental). Requires a common purpose: you have your own reason to go, you choose the destination, and the passengers aren't solicited from the public (AC 61-142)
  • Salesman: an aircraft salesman with at least 200 hours may demonstrate an airplane
  • Charity flights under 91.146 (500 hours)
  • Rescue: search and location operations, with reimbursement allowed
  • Incidental to business or employment, with no passengers or property carried for hire
  • Production flight testing in light-sport aircraft (100 hours PIC in category and class)
  • Towing gliders or unpowered ultralights (61.69, 100 hours PIC in category and class)
IR.I.A.K3

Privileges and limitations of BasicMed

  • IFR is allowed under BasicMed
  • Aircraft: no more than 7 occupants (6 passengers) and maximum certificated takeoff weight up to 12,500 lb (limits raised by the FAA in November 2024)
  • Below 18,000 ft MSL, so no Class A. At or below 250 KIAS
  • In the US only (or a country that has agreed to accept it). Not for compensation or hire
  • Requirements: held an FAA medical after July 14, 2006; CMEC exam by a state-licensed physician every 48 months; online medical education course every 24 calendar months

Requirements in detail — 61.23(c)(3)

  • Hold a valid US driver's license and comply with its restrictions
  • Have held an FAA medical certificate at any time after July 14, 2006 (the most recent one not suspended, revoked, or denied)
  • Complete the online medical education course within the past 24 calendar months
  • Have the Comprehensive Medical Examination Checklist (CMEC) exam by a state-licensed physician within the past 48 months
  • Be under a physician's care for any condition that could affect flight safety
  • Outside the US only where that country accepts BasicMed (for example the Bahamas and Mexico)

References: 14 CFR 61.113(i), 61.23(c)(3), part 68; AC 68-1.

61.53 Self-grounding

Don't act as a crewmember if you know or have reason to know of a medical condition that would make you unable to meet the standards for your medical, or to fly safely. This also applies under BasicMed.

67.401 Special issuance vs. SODA

  • Special issuance: time-limited, re-evaluated. For conditions that can change (for example diabetes)
  • SODA (Statement of Demonstrated Ability): doesn't expire. For static conditions (for example a missing limb)

Over-the-counter medication

  • Check with your AME before flying on anything new
  • FAA guidance: wait at least 5 dosing intervals after the last dose (every 4–6 hours → 30 hours; every 12 hours → 60 hours)
  • Sedating antihistamines (diphenhydramine, the "PM" in many cold remedies) are no-go
  • FAA list: What OTC medications can I take and still be safe to fly?
Risk Management
IR.I.A.R1

Proficiency versus currency

Current means you met 61.57(c). Proficient means you can actually fly a partial-panel approach to minimums in turbulence without falling behind. A pilot who logged 6 approaches in a sim 5 months ago is legal but may not be safe in real IMC.
  • Set a personal proficiency standard tighter than the regulation, such as an approach every month or a session with a CFII every 3 months
  • Use the IPC guidance in the ACS as a self-check before a first real IMC trip after a layoff
  • The same goes for the flight review and passenger currency: 3 landings in 90 days makes you legal, not sharp

IR.I.A.R3

Fitness for flight and physiological factors that might affect flying under instrument conditions

Self-assessment — IM SAFE

  • Illness · Medication · Stress · Alcohol (8 hours bottle to throttle, under .04%, not under the influence: 91.17) · Fatigue · Emotion

Instrument flying raises the stakes on each item. A mild cold can block your sinuses on an IFR descent. Fatigue erodes the scan and makes you more prone to spatial disorientation. OTC antihistamines impair you even when you feel fine: wait 5 dosing intervals and check the FAA's OTC list (medical card).

  • 91.17 No flying within 8 hours of drinking, at 0.04% blood alcohol or more, or while under the influence. No intoxicated passengers except in an emergency
  • 61.53 Self-grounding if you have a condition that makes you unsafe

The full aeromedical list (hypoxia, hyperventilation, ears and sinuses, CO, fatigue, dehydration, scuba diving, alcohol and drugs) is in Aeromedical Factors and ADM. Spatial disorientation and illusions are in IR.IV.A.R2.

IR.I.A.R4

Flying unfamiliar aircraft or operating with unfamiliar flight display systems and avionics

  • Going from a G1000 to a GTN 650 with steam gauges, or adding an unfamiliar autopilot, changes how you load approaches, sequence legs, and spot failures
  • Get training on the specific avionics before flying IFR with them. Practice in VMC or a sim first: loading procedures, activating vectors to final, direct-to, holds, and missed approaches
  • Know the failure modes: what the display shows on AHRS failure, ADC failure, or loss of GPS integrity
  • Reference: AC 90-109A, FAA-H-8083-6 (Advanced Avionics Handbook)

IR.I.A.S1

Apply requirements to act as PIC under IFR in a scenario given by the evaluator

Work the scenarios below. Expect the examiner to hand you a logbook page or a date range and ask whether you are current.

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOToday is November 6. Your last instrument flight was May 20, with 6 approaches, a hold, and tracking. Are you IFR current?

Count back 6 calendar months from the month before the flight: May through October. May 20 is inside the window, so yes, through November 30. On December 1 you are in the grace period and need a safety pilot or a device to regain currency.

SCENARIOYour last 6 HITS were 14 months ago. Can you go up with a safety pilot in VMC and fly 6 approaches to get current?

No. Once you are past the 6-month grace period, only an IPC from a CFII, examiner, or other authorized person restores currency.

SCENARIOA friend with a private certificate but no current medical offers to be your safety pilot. Can she?

No. A safety pilot is a required crewmember, so she needs a current medical (or BasicMed) along with category and class ratings for the airplane.

SCENARIOYou fly the RNAV 6 into Page Field (FMY) in actual IMC and break out at 900 ft, well above the MDA. Can you log the approach for currency?

Yes. You were in actual IMC on the final approach segment, and an FAA interpretation allows logging when you transition to VMC during the final segment. Log the location and type.

SCENARIOYou fly under BasicMed. Can you file IFR at 16,000 ft to clear a line of building cumulus? FL190?

16,000 ft is fine (oxygen rules permitting). FL190 is in Class A, which BasicMed does not allow because it limits you to below 18,000 ft MSL.

SCENARIOYour flight review is due at the end of this month, and your instrument checkride is next week. Do you need a separate flight review?

No. A practical test for a rating satisfies 61.56, so passing the instrument checkride starts a new 24-calendar-month clock.

SCENARIOYou're 45 with a 3rd class medical issued in March 2025. Is it valid for your IFR trip in April 2027?

No. At 40 and over, a 3rd class lasts 24 calendar months, through March 31, 2027. In April 2027 you need a new medical, or you can fly under BasicMed if you meet its requirements.

SCENARIOYou took a nighttime cold medicine with diphenhydramine at 2200. Your IFR departure is at 0700. Fly?

No. Diphenhydramine is dosed every 4–6 hours, so wait about 30 hours (5 dosing intervals). It also impairs you in ways you won't notice until you're behind in IMC.

SCENARIOYour last night landings were 4 months ago. Can you fly an IFR trip that lands at 2200 with a passenger?

Not with the passenger. 61.57(b) needs 3 full-stop landings between 1 hour after sunset and 1 hour before sunrise in the preceding 90 days. Your IFR currency doesn't cover it.

SCENARIOA friend's airline flight to Atlanta is cancelled. He offers to split the costs if you fly him there IFR. Can you?

Only with a common purpose: you have your own reason to go to Atlanta and you'd have gone anyway. Then you may split costs pro rata, paying at least your share. Otherwise it's carrying a passenger for compensation, which a private pilot can't do, instrument rating or not.

What documents must you have with you to act as PIC?

Pilot certificate, medical certificate (or a driver's license under BasicMed), and a government-issued photo ID (61.3).

Acceptable weather products and resources for preflight planning, current and forecast weather, and departure, en route, and arrival planning

Altitude choice: winds and temperatures aloft
Jet stream: a narrow band of fast wind high in the atmosphere, generally west to east, where large temperature differences exist between air masses. It steers storms, is strongest in winter, and has clear air turbulence near it. Coriolis effect: Earth's rotation deflects moving air, so winds aloft flow parallel to the isobars.

Surface analysis and progs (Weather Prediction Center)

  • Surface analysis every 3 hours. 12- and 24-hour progs 4 times a day; 36- and 48-hour progs twice a day
  • Isobars: lines of equal pressure. Close spacing → strong pressure gradient → strong wind
  • Aloft, wind flows parallel to the isobars (Coriolis). At the surface it crosses them at an angle toward low pressure (friction)
  • Northern Hemisphere: wind circulates clockwise out of a high and counterclockwise into a low
  • Trough: elongated low, rising air, clouds and precipitation. Ridge: elongated high, sinking air, generally good weather
  • Fronts, drylines, and outflow boundaries are drawn on the analysis

IR.I.B.K2d

Graphical Forecasts for Aviation (GFA)

NWS tool on aviationweather.gov for the lower 48, surface to FL480. One map shows observations and forecasts from about 14 hours back to 18 hours ahead: clouds (bases and tops), ceilings and visibility, precipitation and weather, thunderstorms, winds, turbulence, icing, freezing level, and G-AIRMETs. It replaced the text area forecast (FA).

IFR use: pick the "Ceiling & Vis" and "Clouds" tabs to see where the IFR is and where tops are, then the "Ice" tab for the freezing level and forecast icing along your altitude.

IR.I.B.K2e

Wind and Temperature Aloft Forecast (FB)

  • Format DDff±tt: direction (true, tens of degrees), speed (kt), temperature (°C)
  • Usually at 3,000, 6,000, 9,000, 12,000, 18,000 ft and up
  • No winds within 1,500 ft of station elevation; no temperatures at the 3,000-ft level or within 2,500 ft of the station
  • 9900 = light and variable (under 5 kt)
  • Speeds 100–199 kt: direction is coded 51–86. Subtract 50 from the direction and add 100 to the speed. 7508 = 250° at 108 kt. 7599 = 250° at 199 kt or more
  • Above FL240 temperatures are always negative and printed without the sign

IFR use: compare groundspeed at each altitude, find where the temperature crosses 0°C (the freezing level), and spot inversions (temperature rising with altitude).

IR.I.B.K2f

Convective Outlook (AC)

From the Storm Prediction Center, probability and severity of convection out to day 8. Risk categories: TSTM (general thunderstorms), MRGL marginal, SLGT slight, ENH enhanced, MDT moderate, HIGH. Day 1 is updated 5 times a day, day 2 twice, day 3 once. Use it a day or two out to decide whether the trip is worth planning.

  • Lifted index: below 0 = unstable (more negative = more unstable). Ask the briefer
  • For IFR: stable air means smooth flying in widespread low ceilings and fog that can be slow to lift. Unstable air means embedded cells you can't see from inside the cloud
IR.I.B.K3b

Wind (windshear, mountain wave, factors affecting wind)

  • Wind results from pressure differences, which come from uneven heating
  • Factors: pressure gradient force (closer isobars = stronger wind), Coriolis (parallel to isobars aloft), surface friction (crosses isobars at the surface)
  • Windshear: a sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance, vertical or horizontal. Found at fronts, low-level temperature inversions, near the jet stream (CAT), and in thunderstorms and microbursts. TAFs show non-convective LLWS with a WS group
  • Mountain wave: stable air (winds as low as about 15 kt) flowing across a ridge sets up waves downwind, with standing lenticular clouds and strong turbulence. Can extend about 100 NM downwind of the Appalachians and far more downwind of the Rockies (AIM 7-6-7). In IMC you won't see the lenticulars
  • Sea breeze (Florida): onshore wind by day as land heats; its convergence line fires afternoon thunderstorms inland
IR.I.B.K3c

Temperature and heat exchange

  • Standard atmosphere at sea level: 29.92 in Hg / 1013.2 mb, 15°C. Standard lapse rate 2°C per 1,000 ft
  • Heat moves by radiation, conduction, convection, and advection. Uneven heating drives all weather: temperature differences → pressure differences → wind
  • Warm air holds more moisture. As air cools it approaches its dew point and clouds or fog form
  • Inversion: temperature rising with altitude. Traps haze, smoke, and fog near the surface; causes LLWS; and a warm layer above a cold surface layer produces freezing rain
Pressure altitude = (29.92 − altimeter setting) × 1,000 + field elevation
ISA temperature at altitude = 15 − (2 × altitude ÷ 1,000)

Cold-temperature error: in very cold air your true altitude is lower than indicated. Some airports publish cold-temperature restricted altitudes (a snowflake icon on the approach chart).

IR.I.B.K3d

Moisture and precipitation

  • Water changes state by evaporation, condensation, sublimation, deposition, melting, and freezing
  • Relative humidity: water vapor present compared with the maximum the air can hold at that temperature. At the dew point the air is saturated
  • Humid air is less dense, so engine and wing performance drop
  • Precipitation needs cloud droplets to grow large enough to fall. Clouds at least 4,000 ft thick usually precede steady precipitation
  • Freezing rain means warmer air (above 0°C) is above you. Ice pellets mean freezing rain is happening at a higher altitude
  • Virga: precipitation that evaporates before reaching the ground. Often marks downdrafts and microbursts

Hydroplaning (landing from an approach in rain)

  • Dynamic: standing water lifts the tire. Minimum speed ≈ 9 × √tire pressure (psi) for a rolling tire, about 7.7 × √psi for a non-rotating tire at touchdown (some references round to 8.6). At 30 psi: about 49 kt rolling, 42 kt at touchdown
  • Viscous: a thin film on a smooth or rubber-coated surface
  • Reverted rubber: a locked wheel heats the water into steam

Land at the slowest safe speed, keep tires inflated, use aerodynamic braking. Grooved runways reduce but don't remove the risk.

IR.I.B.K3i

Icing and freezing level information

Structural icing needs visible moisture and an airframe temperature at or below 0°C.

Effects

  • Lift down (up to about 30%), drag up (as much as 40%), weight up, thrust down
  • Higher stall speed, less maneuverability, possibly no stall warning
  • Heavy ice may make holding altitude impossible

Types

Intensities: trace, light, moderate, severe. Severe = ice protection can't control it; exit immediately.

  • Instrument ice (pitot, static, antennas) · Induction ice (reduces combustion air) · Intake ice (blocks the intake) · Carburetor ice (−7 to +21°C with high humidity, even in clear air)
  • Freezing rain: freezes on impact; warmer air is above. Climbing into the warm layer may help, but ice can build faster than you can climb. Ice pellets mean freezing rain above
  • Freezing level: the altitude where 0°C is. Estimate with the standard lapse rate (2°C per 1,000 ft) or read it from the GFA, G-AIRMET Zulu, or winds aloft temperatures. Below about −20°C most clouds are ice crystals

Getting out

Climb to colder air or above the tops (if close and the airplane can do it), descend below the freezing level (if terrain and MEAs allow), or turn back toward where you weren't icing. Tell ATC: they will usually approve it quickly.

Tailplane stall

The tail has a smaller leading-edge radius and chord, so it collects ice faster than the wing. Signs: lightening control forces, pitch buffeting, a sudden nose-down pitch, often when you extend flaps. Recovery is the opposite of a wing stall: retract flaps, reduce power, pull back. Reference: AC 91-74B.

  • Fog is a cloud that begins within 50 ft of the surface. It forms when the air cools to its dew point or when moisture raises the dew point to the air temperature
  • Watch for a temperature/dew point spread of about 5°C or less and closing, especially at night
  • BR mist: visibility ⅝ SM or more. FG fog: visibility under ⅝ SM

IR.I.B.K3k

Frost

Forms by deposition when the surface is below freezing and below the dew point (clear, cold nights). Frost roughens the wing and disrupts airflow: higher stall speed, less lift (up to 30%), more drag (up to 40%), and a longer takeoff roll. Remove all frost before takeoff. The AFM and FAA guidance say so for every airplane; 91.527 makes it a rule for large and turbine multiengine airplanes.

Risk Management
IR.I.B.R1

Making the go/no-go and continue/divert decisions

R1a · Circumstances that would make diversion prudent

Destination dropping toward minimums, unforecast icing, a line of convection building across your route, fuel burn higher than planned, a system failure (alternator, vacuum, GPS integrity), or pilot fatigue. Decide early, while you still have options and fuel.

R1b · Personal weather minimums

Set them before the flight, above the legal minimums. See IR.I.A.R2. A forecast at your personal minimums at departure time leaves no margin for the forecast being wrong.

R1c · Hazardous weather conditions, including known or forecast icing or turbulence aloft

"Known icing" means icing that is forecast or reported. Without FIKI certification, don't plan to fly into it. Plan an out: a lower altitude above freezing, or an airport behind you that is VFR.

IR.I.B.R2

Use and limitations of weather resources

R2a · Installed onboard weather equipment

Lightning detectors (Stormscope) show electrical discharges, not rain. Onboard radar shows precipitation and suffers attenuation: heavy rain can hide the cell behind it. ADS-B In/FIS-B data is delayed.

R2b · Aviation weather reports and forecasts

Forecasts are least accurate for the timing of fog burning off, frontal passage, and the exact location of convection. Compare the TAF to the current METAR trend and get updates before and during the flight.

R2c · Inflight weather resources

  • ATIS: recorded by controllers and checked for accuracy. Updated hourly, plus specials
  • ASOS: automated observations updated every minute; reports hourly METARs
  • AWOS: levels vary (AWOS-A altimeter only; AWOS-3 adds clouds and visibility, similar to ASOS)
  • Flight Service on 122.2, ATC (centers broadcast SIGMETs, convective SIGMETs, and CWAs), datalink weather, and PIREPs. Ask ATC for PIREPs and give them

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOFebruary, 0600. KFMY: calm wind, clear sky, temp 14°C, dew point 13°C. You plan to depart IFR at 0700 for Orlando. What do you expect?

Radiation fog. Calm wind, clear night, and a 1°C spread are textbook. Check the TAF for when it lifts, think about a takeoff alternate (no Part 91 takeoff minimums, but you need somewhere to go if something fails right after takeoff), or wait until mid-morning.

SCENARIOJuly, 1400. Your ForeFlight NEXRAD shows a gap between two red cells on your route to Tampa. ATC is busy. Do you fly through the gap?

No. Datalink radar can be 15–20 minutes old, the gap may have closed, and you can't see embedded cells in IMC. Ask ATC for a deviation well around the area (20 NM from severe cells), go VFR below if conditions allow, or land and wait. Afternoon sea-breeze storms usually die out by evening.

SCENARIOA G-AIRMET Zulu shows moderate icing from the freezing level at 9,000 ft to 16,000 ft along your route. Your airplane isn't FIKI. Can you go?

That is forecast (known) icing. Go only if you can plan to stay out of it: cruise at 6,000 or 7,000 ft with MEAs that allow it, stay out of clouds above the freezing level, and keep warm air below as an escape. If the MEA or terrain forces you into the icing layer, it's a no-go.

SCENARIOA Convective SIGMET covers a line from Sarasota to Lake Okeechobee. What does that tell you even if it doesn't mention turbulence or icing?

Every convective SIGMET implies severe or greater turbulence, severe icing, and low-level wind shear. Plan around the whole area.

SCENARIOUsing the KRSW TAF in K2c, your ETA at Southwest Florida International is 1600Z. Do you need an alternate?

Yes. The window is 1500–1700Z, and from 1500Z the forecast is broken 800 ft and 5 SM in mist, below 2,000 and 3. Even before 1500Z it's LIFR. Pick an alternate that meets 600-2 or 800-2 at your ETA there.

SCENARIOClimbing out of Gainesville in January, you hear a PIREP: "OVC025-TOP070, TA M02, light rime, during climb." You're filed for 6,000. What do you do?

Ice is reported in the layer you'll cruise in. Options: request 8,000 to get on top (tops 7,000) if you can climb through the layer quickly, request a lower altitude below the cloud base or freezing level if MEAs allow, or don't go. Turn on pitot heat before entering cloud and give your own PIREP.

SCENARIOOn the ILS at 600 ft you notice airspeed climb 15 kt and you balloon above the glideslope, under a dark rain shaft with virga nearby. What's next?

Classic microburst entry: the headwind comes first, then the downdraft and tailwind. Go around now with full power, pitch for best climb, and don't chase the glideslope down. Tell ATC about the windshear (that's a required report).

SCENARIOYou're picking up ice and extend flaps on final. The nose pitches down sharply and the yoke goes light. What happened and how do you recover?

Tailplane stall. Retract the flaps to the previous setting, reduce power, and pull back. That's the opposite of a wing stall recovery. Land with less flap and more speed.

SCENARIOA cold front is forecast to pass Tampa this afternoon. What will you see before, during, and after passage?

Before: falling pressure, southerly winds, building cumulus, possible squall line. During: gusty winds, heavy showers and thunderstorms, a wind shift. After: rising pressure, temperature and dew point drop, northwest winds, clearing skies, gusty and bumpy at low level.

What's the difference between -TSRA, VCSH, and +FC?

−TSRA: thunderstorm with light rain at the station. VCSH: showers in the vicinity (5–10 SM). +FC: tornado or waterspout.

Decode the PIREP: GNV UA /OV GNV270015/TM 1415/FL060/TP PA28/SK OVC025-TOP070/TA M02/IC LGT RIME/RM DURC

Routine PIREP near Gainesville, 15 NM west on the 270° radial, 1415Z, 6,000 ft MSL, a Piper PA-28. Overcast base 2,500, tops 7,000 MSL, temperature −2°C, light rime icing, reported during climb.

In a TAF, what's the difference between FM, BECMG, and TEMPO?

FM: a rapid, lasting change at a specific time. BECMG: a gradual change over a stated period. TEMPO: temporary fluctuations lasting less than an hour each, covering less than half the period.

Decode these winds-aloft groups: 2721+08 at 9,000 ft, 7325+05 at 12,000 ft, and 764248 at FL340.

9,000 ft: 270° at 21 kt, +8°C. 12,000 ft: 73 − 50 = 230°, 25 + 100 = 125 kt, +5°C. FL340: 76 − 50 = 260°, 42 + 100 = 142 kt, −48°C (above FL240 the minus sign is left off).

Field elevation 18 ft, altimeter 30.12. What's the pressure altitude? If it's 14°C, is that standard?

(29.92 − 30.12) × 1,000 + 18 = −182 ft. ISA at sea level is 15°C, so 14°C is about standard.

Which AIRMET covers moderate icing? IFR conditions? Sustained surface winds over 30 kt?

Zulu (freezing). Sierra (sight). Tango (turbulence and strong surface winds, and LLWS).

What three things does a thunderstorm need, and in which stage are its hazards worst?

Moisture, an unstable lapse rate, and lifting action. Hazards are worst in the mature stage, which starts when precipitation reaches the ground.

Name the fog types (UPAIRS). Which two need wind?

Upslope, precipitation-induced, advection, ice, radiation, steam. Upslope and advection need wind.

Rank clear, rime, and mixed ice by typical temperature. Which is most dangerous?

Clear 0 to −10°C, mixed −10 to −15°C, rime −15 to −20°C. Clear ice is the most dangerous: heavy, hard to see, and hard to remove.

You see ice pellets on the windscreen at 4,000 ft. What does that tell you?

Freezing rain is falling somewhere above you: there's a warm layer above and below-freezing air where you are. Get out of the precipitation (turn back, or descend into above-freezing air if there is any). Climbing into the warm layer works only if you can do it quickly. Rare in Florida, but expect it on winter trips north.

Stable or unstable: stratus, steady rain, smooth air, poor visibility?

Stable. Unstable air gives cumulus, showers, turbulence, and good visibility outside the showers.

Your tires are inflated to 30 psi. At what speed could you start to dynamically hydroplane?

9 × √30 ≈ 49 kt for a rolling tire, and about 42 kt (7.7 × √30) for a tire that isn't spinning yet at touchdown. Land at the slowest safe speed on a wet runway.

IR.I.C.K2

IFR cruising altitudes — 91.179

  • In uncontrolled airspace (by magnetic course), below FL290: 0°–179° odd thousands, 180°–359° even thousands. Same pattern for flight levels FL180–FL290
  • RVSM, FL290–FL410: odd FLs eastbound (290, 310…), even FLs westbound (300, 320…)

Minimum IFR altitudes — 91.177

Except for takeoff and landing, no lower than the published minimum altitude for the segment. If none: 2,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 4 NM of course in designated mountainous areas, 1,000 ft elsewhere. You may descend below an MEA to the MOCA, but when navigating by VOR only within 22 NM of the VOR.

Other factors

  • Winds aloft: a lower altitude may give a better groundspeed and keep you below the freezing level
  • Glide distance: over the Gulf or the Everglades, higher altitude buys options after an engine failure
  • Oxygen (91.211): crew above 12,500 to 14,000 ft cabin altitude for more than 30 minutes; crew at all times above 14,000 ft; every occupant offered it above 15,000 ft
IR.I.C.K3

Calculating time, climb and descent rates, course, distance, heading, TAS, groundspeed, ETA, and fuel

K3a–b · Time, rates, course, heading, speeds, ETA including UTC

  • Florida is UTC−5 in standard time (EST) and UTC−4 in daylight time (EDT). Daylight time runs from 0200 on the second Sunday in March to 0200 on the first Sunday in November. The Chart Supplement shows it as UTC−5(−4DT). Flight plans, TAFs, and clearances use Zulu
  • Method: convert departure time to Zulu, add the flight time, convert back. Depart 1430 EDT = 1830Z; + 1:45 = 2015Z (1615 EDT)
  • Climb gradient: Feet per minute = feet per NM × groundspeed ÷ 60 At 90 kt groundspeed, a 300 ft/NM gradient takes 450 fpm
  • 3° descent: VSI ≈ groundspeed × 5 · distance to start ≈ altitude to lose ÷ 300
  • Time = distance ÷ groundspeed. Fuel burned = time (hr) × fuel flow

  • Towered airport: get the clearance from clearance delivery or ground. The plan activates on departure
  • Non-towered airport: get the clearance by phone (Clearance Delivery Phone Number in the Chart Supplement or through FSS), on the ground through an RCO or GCO, or depart VFR and pick it up in the air. Expect a void time or release time
  • Pop-up clearance: request an IFR clearance from ATC in the air without a filed plan, subject to workload

Taking off IFR in Class G without a clearance

91.173 requires a flight plan and clearance only in controlled airspace, so departing into Class G IMC is technically possible. The FAA and NTSB have treated it as careless or reckless operation (91.13) when the pilot entered controlled airspace without a clearance. Don't do it.

Closing

  • Towered airport: closed automatically on landing
  • Non-towered airport: you must close it with ATC or FSS (radio or phone). If you cancel in the air in VMC, you're VFR from that point
  • You can cancel IFR any time you're in VMC and outside Class A airspace
Risk Management
IR.I.C.R1–R4

Pilot, aircraft, environmental, and external-pressure risk — PAVE

  • R1 Pilot: currency, proficiency, IM SAFE, experience with the type of weather forecast
  • R2 Aircraft: IFR equipment working, inspections current, performance and fuel range for the route and alternate
  • R3 enVironment: weather, airports (runway length, approaches available, lighting), airspace, terrain, and obstacles
  • R4 External pressures: a meeting, passengers who need to be home, sunk cost of a rental. Build in a backup (airline ticket, rental car, hotel)
IR.I.C.R5

Limitations of air traffic control (ATC) services

  • Radar coverage is limited at low altitude. Outside it, you give position reports and ATC separates you procedurally
  • ATC radar shows precipitation intensity, not turbulence or icing, and controllers may not call it out when busy
  • In Class E/G and in VMC, you're responsible for seeing and avoiding VFR traffic that ATC isn't talking to
  • A clearance does not guarantee terrain clearance when you accept a direct route below the MVA/MIA or during a visual or contact approach
  • Declaring an emergency ("MAYDAY") gets you priority. "PAN-PAN" signals urgency. "Minimum fuel" is information only

  • Fuel and time estimates are only as good as your performance profile and the winds aloft model
  • Charts and databases expire every 28 or 56 days. Verify currency before every IFR flight
  • Battery and overheating: in a hot Florida cockpit an iPad can shut down. Have a backup (second device, panel GPS, paper)
IR.I.C.R7

Fuel planning

Fuel exhaustion and mismanagement remain leading causes of accidents. Plan the legal minimum plus fuel for what's likely: holding, vectors around weather, a missed approach, and the trip to the alternate. Recompute in flight at each checkpoint: if actual burn or groundspeed is worse than planned, decide on a fuel stop early. Tell ATC "minimum fuel" when you can accept little or no delay, and declare an emergency when you can't.

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOYou're flying KFMY to KORL, ETA 1500Z. The KORL TAF: 1400Z BKN025 5SM, TEMPO 1500–1700Z BKN015 3SM -RA. Do you need an alternate?

Yes. The window is 1400–1600Z, and the TEMPO group brings the ceiling to 1,500 ft, below 2,000. Count the worst conditions in the window, including TEMPO.

SCENARIOYou pick Lakeland (LAL) as the alternate. Its only approaches are RNAV GPS and an ILS. Forecast at ETA: OVC007 3SM. Does it work?

Check the TPP alternate minimums first; if standard, the ILS requires 600-2 and the RNAV requires 800-2. 700 ft and 3 SM meets the precision minimum, so it works if your airplane can fly the ILS. If you planned on the RNAV approach at the alternate, the ceiling is too low.

SCENARIOYour airplane has a non-WAAS IFR GPS. Destination has only RNAV approaches. Your alternate also only has RNAV approaches. Legal?

No. Without WAAS you can rely on GPS at the destination or the alternate, not both. Pick an alternate with a VOR or ILS approach you can fly with your ground-based receivers.

SCENARIOEn route, ATC gives you an unexpected 25-minute hold for a runway closure. You planned to land at the destination with 1 hr 10 min of fuel: 20 min to the alternate plus the 45-minute reserve, plus 5 min extra. What now?

After the hold you would land with 45 min. If the destination then goes bad and you divert, you arrive at the alternate with only 25 min, inside your reserve. Decide now: set a "bingo" time to leave the hold for the alternate (after about 5 min of holding), or go to the alternate right away. Ask for an EFC, tell ATC your fuel state, and say "minimum fuel" if you can accept no further delay.

SCENARIOYou're departing Naples (APF), a towered field, but the tower closes at 2100 and you leave at 2130. How do you get your clearance?

After the tower closes the airport is non-towered (and the surface area may become Class E or G: check the Chart Supplement). Call Clearance Delivery by phone (number in the Chart Supplement) or use an RCO. Expect a void time; if you don't depart by then, notify ATC within 30 minutes.

SCENARIOYou depart Page Field at 1415 EST on a 2 hr 10 min IFR flight. What ETA do you put in the flight plan, in Zulu?

In November Florida is on EST (UTC−5): 1415 EST = 1915Z, plus 2:10 = 2125Z (1625 EST). Flight plans take the departure time in Zulu and the total estimated time en route.

SCENARIOThe briefing lists an FDC NOTAM: "RNAV (GPS) RWY 6 ... LNAV MDA 560 ... TEMPORARY CRANE." What does it mean for you?

The approach minimums are temporarily raised for an obstacle. Use the NOTAM value, not the chart, and recheck whether the destination still meets your personal minimums and whether you need the alternate.

Aircraft Systems Related to IFR Operations

References: FAA-H-8083-2, FAA-H-8083-15, FAA-H-8083-25; AC 91-74 (Pilot Guide: Flight in Icing Conditions); POH/AFM

Knowledge
IR.II.A.K1

Anti-icing and deicing systems: characteristics, operation, and limitations

  • Anti-icing prevents ice from forming: pitot heat, heated windshield, TKS fluid, heated propellers. Turn it on before entering icing
  • Deicing removes ice after it forms: pneumatic boots, electrothermal propeller boots
  • Carburetor heat and alternate air protect the induction system. Carb ice can form from about −7°C to +21°C with high humidity, even in clear air
  • FIKI (flight into known icing) certification is the only approval to fly into forecast or reported icing. Pitot heat and a heated stall vane don't make an airplane FIKI

For the typical IFR trainer

Expect pitot heat, carb heat or alternate air, an alternate static source, and a defroster. That's ice protection for an inadvertent encounter, not approval to fly in icing. Know where each switch is and the AFM limitation that prohibits flight in known icing.

Effects of structural ice

Increases weight and drag, decreases lift and thrust, raises stall speed, and can cause tailplane stall. Don't extend flaps with significant ice if the AFM warns against it: flaps can increase tail downforce and trigger a tailplane stall.

IR.II.A.K2

Flight control systems

  • Autopilot: modes (HDG, NAV/GPS, APR, ALT, VS, IAS/FLC, GS/GP), how to engage and disconnect, and how the AP behaves on a mode reversion
  • Trim: electric trim, runaway trim procedure (disconnect, pull the breaker, fly manually)
  • Flight director: command bars to fly a selected heading, course, or vertical mode, with or without the autopilot engaged
  • Know the preflight test for your autopilot and the disconnect methods: AP button, yoke disconnect switch, circuit breaker, and master switch as a last resort
Pilot 100i

IFR systems in the PPL guide's airplane

From the Piper Pilot 100i numbers used in the PPL guide. Verify against the POH for the airplane you test in.

A good systems answer covers four things: how it's powered, how you recognize a failure, what you do, and what else it takes down.

Approval and equipment

  • KOEL approves day and night VFR and IFR, non-icing (POH 2-6). Not approved for flight into known icing. The G3X limitations section (POH 2-17) lists the equipment required for IFR
  • G3X Touch: PFD, engine indications, FliteCharts, SafeTaxi. Fed by an ADAHRS (AHRS for attitude and heading, ADC for airspeed, altitude, and vertical speed). No vacuum system
  • G5 standby: its own AHRS and air data, up to about 4 hours on its internal battery (POH 3-23)
  • GNX 375: WAAS GPS navigator with ADS-B In/Out transponder
  • Anti-ice: pitot heat (on before visible moisture near freezing), defroster, alternate air for the induction system

GFC 500 autopilot (POH 2-18, 7-21)

  • Off for takeoff and landing. The preflight test must pass
  • Minimum engagement height 800 ft AGL, or 200 ft AGL on an approach. Engage between 65 and 140 KIAS
  • Disconnect: red A/P DISC switch, both halves of the trim switch, or the AP key. Abnormal: pull the AUTOPILOT breaker
  • Max fuel imbalance with the autopilot engaged: 10 gal

Electrical system (POH 7-33)

  • 28 V: 24 V battery and a 70 A belt-driven alternator. The regulator takes the alternator offline above 32 V
  • Bus 1: alternator, PFD, ADAHRS, engine sensors, GPS/transponder, nav/datalink, COM 1, COM 2/audio, standby instrument. Bus 2: autopilot, pitch trim, lights, pitot heat, fuel pump, vent fan, USB
  • Alternator failure: ALT annunciation, ammeter at 0, voltage dropping toward 24. The engine keeps running on its magnetos and engine-driven fuel pump. The G5 keeps going on its own battery. Tell ATC and get on the ground

Alternate static (POH 7-40)

Uses cabin air. Turn the vent fan off, close the vents, and turn on heat and defrost. Altimeter error is under 50 ft.

Heat and carbon monoxide

Cabin heat comes from a muff around the exhaust. A cracked exhaust can put CO in the cabin: a winter IFR hazard. See aeromedical.

Risk Management
IR.II.A.R1

Operations in icing conditions

If you pick up ice: turn on pitot heat and defrost, tell ATC, and request a change (lower to warmer air, higher toward tops, or a reversal). Don't wait for the ice to get worse. Fly a faster approach, avoid abrupt maneuvers, and plan a longer landing distance.

IR.II.A.R2

Limitations of anti-icing and deicing systems

Systems protect only certain surfaces: unprotected areas (struts, antennas, gear) still accumulate ice. TKS fluid runs out; boots leave residual ice; heated windshields may only clear a small panel. Severe icing exceeds the capability of any certified system.

IR.II.A.R3

Use of automated systems in instrument conditions

  • The autopilot reduces workload. Use it in IMC so you have time to brief, copy, and plan
  • Know what it's doing: call out the armed and active modes on the annunciator. "What's it doing now?" is a warning sign
  • Some autopilots disconnect or trim against icing without telling you. When ice accumulates, disconnect periodically, hold the yoke, and feel for trim changes
  • Keep hand-flying skills sharp: you will need them when the autopilot fails

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOClimbing through 9,000 ft in cloud over north Florida in January, OAT −2°C, you see a thin ridge of ice on the wing leading edge. What do you do?

Pitot heat on (should already be on in visible moisture near freezing), tell ATC you're picking up light rime and request a lower altitude where the OAT is above freezing. Don't keep climbing into colder cloud hoping for tops unless you know they're close. Expect higher stall speed: fly the approach faster and consider landing with less flap if the POH allows.

SCENARIOThe autopilot is holding altitude and the trim indicator keeps moving nose-up. Airspeed is slowly decaying. What's happening?

Something is adding drag or reducing lift, possibly ice, while the autopilot trims to hold altitude. The autopilot can trim the airplane right into a stall and then disconnect. Disconnect it, fly by hand, add power, and get out of the conditions.

SCENARIOIn IMC in the Pilot 100i, the ALT annunciator lights and voltage reads 24.1. What do you do, and how long do you have?

Fly the airplane, run the alternator failure checklist, and shed load: about 30 minutes of battery remains. Tell ATC now while COM 1 works, ask for vectors to the nearest airport with an approach you can fly, and consider declaring an emergency. The engine keeps running; the G5 has its own battery for attitude, airspeed, and altitude.

Name three ways to disconnect your autopilot.

The AP disconnect button on the yoke, the AP button on the control panel, and the autopilot circuit breaker. Pulling the electric trim breaker or avionics master also works on many installations, but know what else it takes down.

Use of an electronic flight bag (EFB), if used

  • Part 91 operators may use an EFB as a replacement for paper charts without FAA authorization (AC 91-78) as long as the data is current and the device works for the whole flight

Risk Management
IR.II.B.R1

Failure to monitor and manage automated systems

Typical traps: the GPS still in OBS mode so it won't sequence, the CDI source set to GPS when you meant to fly the localizer, approach mode armed but not active, and an altitude preselect left at the wrong value. Verbalize every mode change and check the annunciator.

IR.II.B.R2

Difference between approved and non-approved navigation devices

Approved: installed per its STC or type certificate (TSO-C129, C145, C146 GPS) with an AFM supplement that permits IFR use. Non-approved: handheld GPS, tablet GPS, and EFB moving maps. They're backups for situational awareness and can't be used to fly an approach or as primary navigation.

IR.II.B.R3

Failure to manage flight modes and navigation instruments, and their failures

Know what's lost with each failure: an AHRS failure takes attitude off the PFD (fly the standby); an ADC failure removes airspeed, altitude, and VSI tapes; a GPS loss of integrity ("LOI") during an LPV approach may downgrade to LNAV or require a missed approach. Have a plan for each one before you need it.

IR.II.B.R4

Use of an EFB

Distraction (heads down zooming charts in IMC), relying on the EFB's own-ship position, and outdated charts are the main hazards. Set the chart up before the approach, the same way you'd fold a paper plate.

IR.II.B.R5

Use of navigation databases

Check the database effective date during preflight. A database error or an approach that differs from the chart can lead you off course: compare the loaded approach (waypoints, altitudes, courses) with the chart before you begin.

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIODescending in heavy rain, the airspeed slowly increases while the altimeter and VSI look normal, then the airspeed rises in a climb. What failed and what do you do?

Pitot inlet and drain blocked: the trapped air makes the ASI act like an altimeter. Turn on pitot heat, fly pitch and power by known settings, and cross-check with groundspeed from the GPS.

SCENARIOIn cruise the altimeter is stuck at 6,000 ft and the VSI reads zero during a climb ATC requested. What happened?

Static blockage. Open the alternate static source, expect slightly high altimeter and airspeed readings (check the POH correction), and tell ATC.

SCENARIOYour VOR check was 34 days ago. Your GPS is WAAS. Can you go IFR?

Not if you will use the VOR for IFR navigation. 91.171 requires the check within 30 days to use the VOR receiver under IFR. You could do a check on the ramp (VOT or ground checkpoint) or a dual-VOR check before departure. Any ILS/LOC use doesn't need the 91.171 check.

SCENARIOYour GPS database expired yesterday. Can you fly the RNAV (GPS) approach at your destination?

No. GPS approaches must come from a current database. En route and terminal use is allowed if you verify the waypoints against current charts, but plan a ground-based approach or update the database.

How many satellites does RAIM need, and when do you need to check it?

5 for fault detection (or 4 plus baro-aiding), 6 for exclusion. With a non-WAAS GPS, check RAIM prediction before relying on GPS for approaches. With WAAS, check WAAS NOTAMs instead.

Then it must be removed (with W&B and logbook updated) or deactivated and placarded "Inoperative," and a pilot or mechanic must determine it isn't a hazard. If the item is required, you need a special flight permit (21.197) to move the airplane.

MEL and KOEL

  • MEL: an FAA-approved list, specific to one aircraft, of equipment that may be inoperative under stated conditions. If the airplane has one, follow it instead of 91.213(d)
  • KOEL: in the POH limitations section, lists equipment required for each kind of operation (day VFR, night VFR, IFR, icing). This is where IFR-required items beyond 91.205(d) show up, such as a standby attitude source

Inoperative approach components

The TPP Inoperative Components table and the chart notes tell you how minimums change, for example when the approach lighting system is out (visibility increases). An inoperative glideslope means you fly the LOC minimums.

Risk Management
IR.II.C.R1

Operating with inoperative equipment

Legal isn't the same as wise. A dead autopilot is legal for IFR in most trainers, but single-pilot IMC by hand in convective weather is a heavy load. An inoperative standby attitude indicator is legal if not required, but removes your backup for an AHRS or vacuum failure. Adjust your personal minimums for each missing item.

IR.II.C.R2

Operating with outdated navigation publications or databases

Approaches, altitudes, and frequencies change every cycle. An outdated chart may show a decommissioned NAVAID, a step-down altitude that was raised for a new tower, or an old missed approach. 91.103 makes you responsible for knowing all available information.

Skills
IR.II.C.S1

Perform a preflight inspection per the checklist and determine the airplane is airworthy for IFR

Expect the examiner to ask you to show the IFR inspections in the logbooks: annual, altimeter/static and transponder dates, ELT, VOR check record, and AD compliance. Tab them before the checkride.

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOThe 91.411 altimeter/static check was done 25 calendar months ago. Can you fly IFR today?

Not in controlled airspace. You could fly VFR, or IFR only in Class G (which isn't realistic). Get the check done.

SCENARIOOn the ramp the altimeter shows 60 ft above field elevation with the current setting. Is it usable?

Yes. The AIM tolerance is ±75 ft. If it's off by more than 75 ft, have it checked before IFR flight.

SCENARIOThe clock is inoperative but you have a GPS with a digital timer on the MFD. Legal for IFR?

91.205(d) requires a clock installed as part of the aircraft equipment that displays hours, minutes, and seconds. If the panel-mounted GPS/MFD timer meets that, many operators accept it. A phone or watch does not. Check the equipment list and your POH, then apply 91.213 to the broken clock.

SCENARIOThe heading indicator precesses 15° every 10 minutes. The airplane has no MEL. Can you go IFR?

A heading indicator is required by 91.205(d), and one that precesses that much is effectively inoperative for IFR. No IFR flight until it's repaired.

SCENARIOThe annual expired yesterday and the airplane is at another airport. You need it back for an IFR lesson. Options?

It can't be flown on its own authority. Get a special flight permit (21.197/21.199) through the FSDO or a DAR to ferry it to a shop, then have the annual done. A ferry permit won't cover an IFR training flight.

SCENARIOThe registration in the airplane shows an expiration date of last month. Can you fly?

No. Registration expires every 7 years (47.40). An unregistered airplane can't be flown, and its airworthiness certificate is effective only while it's registered in the US (21.181). The owner must renew it.

Who is responsible for airworthiness: you or the owner?

Both. The owner or operator maintains it in an airworthy condition (91.403); the PIC decides whether it's airworthy before each flight (91.7).

SCENARIOThe landing light is out on a daytime IFR rental flight that's not for hire. Go or no-go?

A landing light is required only at night when flying for hire (91.205(c)). Check the KOEL. If not required: deactivate, placard "INOP," confirm it's not a hazard, and go.

Knowledge
IR.III.A.K1

Elements and procedures related to ATC clearances and pilot/controller responsibilities, including void times

Clearance format — C RAFT

  • Clearance limit · Route · Altitude (initial and expected) · Frequency (departure) · Transponder code
Cessna 123AB, cleared to Orlando Executive airport via the LBV7 departure, radar vectors LBV, then as filed. Climb and maintain 3,000, expect 6,000 one-zero minutes after departure. Departure frequency 119.2, squawk 4521.

Read back the clearance in full. ATC must read it correctly back to you only when you ask; it's your job to catch errors. "Cleared as filed" covers the route only: you still get an altitude.

Clearance timing terms (AIM 5-2-7)

  • Void time: if not off the ground by then, the clearance is void. Notify ATC within 30 minutes after the void time that you didn't depart
  • Hold for release: you may not depart IFR until released
  • Release time: the earliest time you may depart
  • EDCT (expect departure clearance time): issued by traffic management; depart within ±5 minutes

Common clearance phrases

  • "Climb via"/"descend via": fly the SID/STAR's lateral path and published altitude and speed restrictions. Deletes previously assigned speed restrictions; published ones still apply
  • "Climb and maintain"/"descend and maintain": start promptly. Climb at an optimum rate to 1,000 ft below the assigned altitude, then 500–1,500 fpm (AIM 4-4-10)
  • "At pilot's discretion": start when you like, but once you leave an altitude you can't go back to it
  • "Expect": planning information only, not a clearance. Used in lost comm
  • "Cruise 5,000": a block from the minimum IFR altitude up to 5,000. You may climb and descend within it, but once you report leaving an altitude you can't return to it. Also clears you for any approach at the destination

Pilot and controller responsibilities

  • Pilot: comply with clearances (91.123), request amendment if unable, see and avoid in VMC, terrain clearance once you accept a visual or contact approach or a VFR-on-top clearance
  • Controller: separation from other IFR traffic in controlled airspace, safety alerts, traffic advisories when workload permits

Risk Management
IR.III.A.R1

Failure to fully understand an ATC clearance

If any part of a clearance is unclear, ask: "Say again the route after LBV." Don't fly what you think they said. Write it down in C RAFT order and verify the route against the chart before taxiing.

IR.III.A.R2

Inappropriate, incomplete, or incorrect ATC clearances

Controllers make mistakes: an altitude below the MEA, a vector toward terrain or a towering cell, a clearance missing an altitude. Ask for clarification and say "unable" when needed. The PIC has final authority (91.3).

IR.III.A.R3

ATC clearance inconsistent with aircraft performance or navigation capability

Examples: an RNAV SID when your GPS isn't approved for it, a climb gradient of 400 ft/NM your trainer can't make on a hot day, a speed of 160 kt on final. Respond "unable" and give an alternative. File the correct equipment code and add "NO SIDS" in remarks if needed.

IR.III.A.R4

ATC clearance intended for other aircraft with similar call signs

N123AB and N123AD on the same frequency is a real trap. Always read back with your full call sign. If you aren't sure the call was for you, ask: "Was that for 123AB?" ATC may say "caution, similar call signs."

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIODeparting Punta Gorda (PGD) with the tower closed. Clearance: "Clearance void if not off by 1430Z." At 1428Z you're still holding short waiting for traffic to clear the runway. What now?

Don't take off after 1430Z on that clearance. Call ATC (phone or RCO) for a new void time. If you can't reach them, you must still tell ATC within 30 minutes after the void time that you didn't depart, or they'll start search procedures.

SCENARIOCleared "cruise 5,000" to Arcadia (X06), an uncontrolled airport with an RNAV approach. You're at 5,000 and descend to 3,000, reporting it. Then you see cloud ahead at 3,000. Can you climb back to 4,000?

No. Once you've reported leaving an altitude in the cruise block, you can't return to it without a new clearance. Ask ATC. The cruise clearance does let you start any approach at Arcadia without a separate approach clearance.

SCENARIOApproach says "Descend and maintain 2,000." You're on a route segment with an MEA of 3,000 shown on the chart. What do you do?

Radar controllers can assign altitudes down to the MVA, which may be lower than the MEA. That's legal if you're in radar contact. If you're unsure (for example, you're about to lose radar or communications), confirm with ATC: "Confirm 2,000 for 123AB?"

SCENARIOYour alternator fails in IMC. What reports are required and what's the first thing you do?

Fly the airplane, then shed electrical load. Report the failure to ATC (91.187 requires reporting a malfunction of navigation, approach, or communication equipment). Request vectors to the nearest suitable airport with VFR or an approach you can fly before the battery dies. Consider declaring an emergency.

When must you report "leaving" an altitude?

Every time you vacate a previously assigned altitude or flight level for a newly assigned one, whether in or out of radar contact (AIM 5-3-3).

Timing and turns

  • Inbound leg: 1 minute at or below 14,000 ft, 1½ minutes above. Adjust the outbound leg to get it
  • Start outbound timing over or abeam the fix, whichever is later. If you can't tell when you're abeam, start at the completion of the outbound turn

  • Turns: 3°/sec, or 30° bank, or 25° with a flight director, whichever needs less bank
  • Wind: triple the inbound wind correction angle on the outbound leg

Entries (based on heading to the fix, relative to the holding course)

  • Direct (180° sector): cross the fix, turn in the holding direction to the outbound leg
  • Teardrop (70° sector on the holding side): cross the fix, turn to a heading 30° (or less) from the outbound course into the holding side, fly 1 minute, turn in the holding direction to intercept inbound
  • Parallel (110° sector on the non-holding side): cross the fix, turn to parallel the holding course outbound on the non-holding side for 1 minute, turn back toward the holding side to intercept inbound (or go direct to the fix)

Quick math (heading minus inbound course, from −180° to +180°): for right turns, −70° to +110° is direct, beyond +110° is teardrop, beyond −70° is parallel. For left turns flip the signs: −110° to +70° direct, beyond −110° teardrop, beyond +70° parallel.

Standard holds use right turns. Nonstandard holds use left turns. The entry must keep you in protected airspace; slight differences in entry choice near sector boundaries are acceptable (±5°).

Turn · Time · Twist (set the course) · Throttle · Talk

IR.III.B.R1

Recalculating fuel reserves if assigned an unanticipated EFC time

At the hold, compute: fuel on board minus fuel to destination, plus fuel to the alternate, plus 45 minutes. What's left is your holding fuel. Set a "bingo" time to leave for the alternate and tell ATC well before it.

IR.III.B.R2

Scenarios and circumstances that could result in minimum fuel or the need to declare an emergency

  • Minimum fuel: you can accept little or no delay. Not an emergency, gives no priority, but informs ATC
  • Emergency: you will land with less than the planned reserve or can't reach your destination with it. Declare it and get priority
IR.III.B.R3

Scenarios that could lead to holding, including deteriorating weather at the planned destination

Destination below minimums, a runway closure, traffic flow programs, or an aircraft disabled on the runway. Holding while waiting for weather to improve is reasonable only with fuel and a better alternate in reserve.

IR.III.B.R4

Holding entry and wind correction while holding

Visualize the hold before you reach the fix: draw it on the chart or use the EFB's hold depiction. A wrong entry or forgetting wind correction can blow you out of protected airspace, especially with a strong wind on the non-holding side.

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOYou're heading 090° direct to a VOR. ATC: "Hold west of the VOR on the 270 radial, standard turns." What entry?

The inbound course is 090°, which matches your heading, so you're arriving along the inbound course from the holding side: a direct entry. Cross the VOR, turn right to 270° outbound.

SCENARIOHeading 360° to a fix. "Hold east of the fix on the 090° radial, left turns." Which entry?

Parallel. The inbound course is 270°, and left turns put the pattern south of the inbound leg. Your heading is 90° right of the inbound course, which is more than 70° to the right, so it falls in the parallel sector for left turns. Cross the fix, turn right to 090° and fly outbound on the north (non-holding) side for 1 minute, then turn right, toward the holding side, more than 180° to intercept the inbound course.

SCENARIOInbound leg in a hold took 1 min 20 sec. How do you correct the outbound leg?

Inbound is 20 seconds too long, so shorten the outbound leg by about 20 seconds.

SCENARIOYou're holding at 4,000 ft with an EFC 40 minutes away. You have 1 hr 40 min of fuel. The alternate is 30 minutes away. What's your bingo time?

You need 30 min to the alternate + 45 min reserve = 1 hr 15 min (plus time for an approach). That leaves about 25 minutes of holding, less a margin for the approach and missed approach at destination. You can't wait for the EFC: tell ATC now that you'll need to divert in 15–20 minutes if not cleared for the approach.

What do you say to ATC when you arrive at the holding fix?

Callsign, fix, and altitude (plus time when not in radar contact): "123AB, LBV, 4,000." Then report again when leaving the fix.

Risk Management
IR.IV.A.R1

Instrument flying hazards, including failure to maintain a cross-check, fixation, and physiological factors

Fatigue, hypoxia, stress, and medications all slow the scan. Fixation on a misbehaving GPS or a chart while the airplane slowly banks is a classic setup for a graveyard spiral. Fly the airplane first, and use the autopilot to free up attention.

Hypoxia types

  • Hypoxic: low partial pressure of O2 at altitude
  • Hypemic: blood can't carry O2 (CO poisoning, anemia, blood donation)
  • Stagnant: poor circulation (G-forces, cold)
  • Histotoxic: cells can't use O2 (alcohol, drugs)

Hyperventilation (common when anxious in IMC) has similar symptoms: slow your breathing or talk out loud. More in Aeromedical Factors and ADM.

IR.IV.A.R2

Spatial disorientation and optical illusions

Vestibular illusions

  • The leans: after a slow, prolonged turn, leveling the wings feels like banking the other way
  • Coriolis: moving your head during a prolonged turn (reaching for a chart) produces a strong sensation of rotation on another axis. The most overwhelming one
  • Graveyard spiral: in a prolonged turn you feel level, notice altitude loss, and pull back, tightening the spiral
  • Graveyard spin: recovering from a spin, you feel a spin the other way and re-enter the original one
  • Somatogravic: rapid acceleration feels like pitching up (pilots push nose-down on a night go-around); deceleration feels like pitching down
  • Inversion: an abrupt change from climb to level feels like tumbling backward
  • Elevator: an updraft feels like climbing; a downdraft feels like descending

Visual and optical illusions

  • False horizon (sloping clouds, ground lights) and autokinesis (a fixed light seems to move)
  • Narrow runway: looks too high (you fly low). Wide: looks too low
  • Upsloping runway or terrain: looks too high (you fly low)
  • Featureless terrain / black hole: over water or dark land at night, you feel higher than you are and descend low. Common on night approaches over the Gulf or into Everglades airports
  • Rain on the windscreen: you feel higher. Haze: runway looks farther away
  • Fog: entering fog can feel like pitching up
  • Bright runway lights: the runway looks closer

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIONight departure from Naples over the Gulf. Right after you raise the flaps and accelerate, you feel the nose pitching up steeply. The attitude indicator shows 7° nose up. What's happening?

SCENARIOIn a long, shallow turn in IMC you notice altitude dropping and pull back, but the descent increases and the airspeed rises. What is this and how do you fix it?

A graveyard spiral. Level the wings first using the attitude indicator, then raise the nose and reduce power. Pulling back while banked only tightens the spiral.

What bank angle gives a standard-rate turn at 110 KTAS?

(110 ÷ 10) × 1.5 = 16.5°, roughly 17°.

Name the three fundamental instrument flying skills and the three common scan errors.

Cross-check, instrument interpretation, and aircraft control. Errors: fixation, omission, and emphasis.

SCENARIOThe examiner hands you the airplane: airspeed rising quickly, altimeter unwinding, turn coordinator showing a right turn. What do you do?

Airspeed is decaying toward a stall. Power and lowering the nose together stop the decay; leveling the wings next reduces the load factor.

  • Tracking: bracket. Find a heading that holds the needle, then refine wind correction in halves

DME arc

  • Turn 90° to the radial about ½ NM before the arc (lead depends on groundspeed: ≈ 0.5% of groundspeed in NM)
  • Fly the arc with headings tangent to it: turn 10°–20° toward the station each time the radial moves 10°–20°. With an RMI or bearing pointer, keep it on the wingtip reference
  • Stay within ±1 NM of the arc. Watch for the lead radial (LR) to turn inbound

VOR time and distance

Minutes to station = seconds for bearing change ÷ degrees of change
Distance off course ≈ 200 ft per dot per NM from the VOR

Station checks and limitations: see IR.II.B.K2.

IR.V.A.K2

Satellite-based navigation: orientation, course determination, equipment, tests, regulations, interference, appropriate use, limitations, and RAIM/WAAS and databases

  • GPS CDI shows cross-track distance in NM, not angle. Scaling changes en route (2 NM) → terminal (1 NM) → approach (0.3 NM or angular)
  • OBS (suspend) mode stops automatic waypoint sequencing: required to fly some holds and course reversals manually. Forgetting to un-suspend is a common error
  • Interference: GPS testing and jamming are published in GPS NOTAMs. Report suspected interference to ATC. Have a ground-based backup plan
  • RAIM (non-WAAS) and WAAS integrity: if integrity is lost, the receiver annunciates and you must revert to another navigation source or fly the missed approach

RNAV basics

  • T-routes (1,200 AGL to 18,000 MSL) and Q-routes (FL180–FL450) are RNAV 2 unless charted otherwise
  • RNP = RNAV with onboard performance monitoring and alerting. En route RNP 2.0, terminal RNP 1.0, approach RNP 0.3 (95% of the time)
  • "RNAV (RNP)" approaches require special authorization (AR). Most US "RNAV (GPS)" approaches don't
Risk Management
IR.V.A.R1

Management of automated navigation and autoflight systems

Know what the autopilot is coupled to. A "NAV" mode coupled to a GPS that's still flying the old flight plan, or a CDI on VLOC while the GPS has the course, will fly you confidently the wrong way. Confirm the source and annunciators after every change.

IR.V.A.R2

Distractions, task prioritization, loss of situational awareness, or disorientation

Program the box when the workload is low (in cruise, on vectors). If you're behind, ask ATC for a vector or delay, or go to a simpler mode (heading and altitude) until you catch up.

IR.V.A.R3

Limitations of the navigation system in use

VOR line of sight and service volume, NDB errors near thunderstorms and at dusk (night effect), localizer false courses outside the coverage area, and GPS integrity outages.

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOYou're on the 090 radial of a VOR. ATC says "intercept the 045 radial outbound." What do you set and which way do you turn?

Set 045 with a FROM indication. You're on the 090 radial, which is clockwise (south and east) of 045, so the needle will be deflected toward the 045 radial. Turn to a heading of about 015°–000° (30°–45° intercept angle relative to 045, pointing toward the radial), then turn to 045 when the needle centers.

SCENARIOYour GPS shows "LOI" while flying a T-route in IMC. What do you do?

Loss of integrity: the GPS position can't be trusted for navigation. Tell ATC, ask for vectors or switch to VOR navigation, and check whether a GPS NOTAM covers the area.

SCENARIOA bearing to a VOR changes 10° in 2 minutes. How far are you from the station at 120 kt TAS?

Time to station = 120 seconds ÷ 10° = 12 minutes. Distance = 120 kt × 12 ÷ 60 = 24 NM.

How much should you lead the turn onto a 10 DME arc at 120 kt groundspeed?

About 0.5% of groundspeed: 0.6 NM. Start the turn at about 10.6 DME when inbound to the arc.

Departure, En Route, and Arrival Operations

Knowledge
IR.V.B.K1

ATC routes, including departure procedures and climb gradients, en route, and STARs with altitude and speed constraints

Takeoff minimums — 91.175(f)

No takeoff minimums for Part 91. For Parts 121, 125, 129, and 135, if none are published: 1 SM for airplanes with 1 or 2 engines, ½ SM for more than 2. A "T" symbol on the chart means nonstandard takeoff minimums or a departure procedure: look in the front of the TPP.

Obstacle protection on departure

  • Cross the departure end of the runway (DER) at least 35 ft above it
  • Climb to 400 ft above the DER before turning
  • Climb at least 200 ft/NM (or the published gradient) to the minimum IFR altitude
  • The 200 ft/NM standard gives 48 ft/NM of clearance above a 40:1 (152 ft/NM) obstacle surface
Feet per minute = feet per NM × groundspeed ÷ 60

Departure procedure types

  • Diverse departure: no ODP published means the runway meets diverse departure criteria. Climb on runway heading to 400 ft, then turn on course climbing at 200 ft/NM
  • ODP (obstacle departure procedure): obstacle clearance only. Text or graphic (titled "OBSTACLE"). You may fly it without being assigned
  • SID: obstacle clearance and ATC simplification. Always graphic. Fly it only when cleared. Put "NO SIDS" in remarks to avoid them
  • VCOA (visual climb over airport): climb in visual conditions over the airport to the published altitude, then proceed. Tell ATC early
  • DVA (diverse vector area): lets ATC vector you below the MVA/MIA right after takeoff
  • RNAV DPs require RNAV 1. "Radar Required" departures depend on ATC vectors

En route

Victor airways and T-routes, MEAs and MOCAs, changeover points, and reporting points. See IFR altitudes.

STARs

  • Transition from the en route structure to a point where an approach can begin. Named for the fix where the basic procedure starts
  • Accepting a STAR means you must fly it. Put "NO STARS" in remarks to avoid them
  • Maintain your last assigned altitude until cleared to descend (or "descend via")
  • "Descend via" lets you descend at your discretion while meeting all published altitude and speed restrictions
IR.V.B.K2

Pilot/controller responsibilities, communication procedures, and ATC services available to pilots

  • ATC services: IFR separation, radar vectors, traffic advisories, safety alerts (terrain/obstruction and aircraft conflict), weather information, emergency assistance
  • Pilot: tell ATC when unable, read back altitudes and headings, report required items, follow the clearance unless an amendment is received
  • Minimum vectoring altitude (MVA): may be lower than the MEA. ATC can assign it in radar contact
  • "Radar contact lost" or "radar service terminated": resume position reports

IR.V.B.R1

ATC communications and compliance with published procedures

Unclear or missed transmissions, frequency congestion, and accepting a procedure you can't fly (climb gradient, equipment). Brief every DP and STAR before you accept it.

IR.V.B.R2

ADS-B In shows aircraft that broadcast ADS-B or are relayed through TIS-B (only when you're within ground station and radar coverage). Aircraft without transponders don't appear at all. Displayed positions can lag.

IR.V.B.R3

Responsibility to "see and avoid" when in VMC

91.113(b): when weather permits, every pilot must see and avoid other aircraft, even on an IFR clearance. In Class E between layers, VFR traffic not talking to ATC can be right where you are.

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOAn ODP requires 320 ft/NM to 1,200 ft. On a hot day you expect a 75 kt climb groundspeed. What climb rate do you need? Can your airplane do it?

320 × 75 ÷ 60 = 400 fpm. Check the POH climb chart at that density altitude and weight. If you can't make it, wait for cooler air, take less fuel or passengers, or depart in VMC using the VCOA if one is published.

SCENARIOThe airport has no ODP and you're cleared "fly runway heading, climb and maintain 3,000." Tower hands you to departure, which is busy. When can you turn?

You were assigned runway heading, so hold it until ATC gives you a vector or turn, even above 400 ft. The "400 ft before turning" rule matters when you're turning on your own (diverse departure or ODP).

SCENARIOYou're cleared on a STAR and given "descend via." The first fix shows "at or above 8,000," the next "11,000–9,000." You're at 12,000. Where can you descend?

Descend at your discretion to meet each restriction: you may start down from 12,000, must be between 11,000 and 9,000 at the second fix, and must not be below 8,000 at the first. Never descend below a published minimum segment altitude.

What's the difference between an ODP and a SID?

An ODP provides only obstacle clearance and you can fly it without ATC assigning it. A SID adds ATC traffic flow and communication benefits, is always graphic, and is flown only when assigned.

LNAV versus LP

  • LNAV: lateral guidance with ±0.3 NM linear CDI scaling. Any IFR-approved GPS (WAAS not needed)
  • LP: WAAS-only, angular lateral guidance like a localizer, usually published where obstacles prevent vertical guidance. Lower MDA than LNAV. LP and LPV are never published on the same chart
  • "LNAV+V": advisory glidepath. Still non-precision: the MDA is a hard floor

When can you descend? (AIM 5-5-4, 91.175(i))

  • When cleared for the approach and established on a segment of a published route or approach, descend to that segment's minimum altitude
  • On vectors or an unpublished route: maintain the last assigned altitude until established

Procedure turn (91.175(j), AIM 5-4-9)

  • A course reversal to get established inbound. Stay within the charted distance (usually 10 NM) and at or above the PT altitude. Max 200 KIAS
  • The PT shape is your choice unless a teardrop or hold-in-lieu is charted; the direction and distance limits are mandatory
  • Do not fly the PT when — SHARP TT: Straight-in approach clearance, Holding in lieu of PT (already flown), Arc (DME arc) or route marked NoPT, Radar vectors to final, No PT depicted, Timed approach from a holding fix, Teardrop course reversal charted (fly the teardrop instead)

Descending below the MDA — 91.175(c)

  1. Continuously in a position to descend to land on the intended runway at a normal rate with normal maneuvers (and, for Part 121/135, within the touchdown zone)
  2. Flight visibility is not less than the visibility on the approach chart
  3. At least one of the visual references for the runway is distinctly visible and identifiable. See the list

Visual descent point (VDP) and vertical descent angle (VDA)

  • VDP ("V" on the profile): the point from which a normal descent from the MDA can start. Don't descend below MDA before it. If you can't identify it, fly as if none is published
  • Calculate one: VDP (NM from threshold) = height above touchdown ÷ 300 or subtract (HAT ÷ 10) seconds from the FAF-to-MAP time
  • VDA: an advisory 3°-ish path from the FAF to the threshold crossing height. Doesn't guarantee obstacle clearance below the MDA. If the chart says VDA not authorized, don't use it below the MDA

Missed approach point

VOR/NDB/LOC without a waypoint: timed from the FAF (table at the bottom of the chart) or a DME fix. RNAV: the MAP waypoint, usually the threshold.

Usually yes: visibility minimums increase. Check the chart notes and the Inoperative Components table in the TPP front matter.

Go missed when (91.175(e), AIM 5-5-5)

  • You reach the DA or MAP without the required visual references, or a safe landing isn't possible
  • You lose visual references below the MDA or DA while circling or landing
  • ATC tells you to
  • Your navigation fails, the approach becomes unstable, or something else makes continuing unsafe

  • GPS: after the MAP, the box usually suspends sequencing. Press OBS/SUSP or "activate missed approach" to sequence the missed approach legs. Know your unit's procedure
  • Autopilot: arm GA (go-around) mode if equipped, then re-engage NAV and the appropriate vertical mode

  • S7 If a missed approach occurs, turn in the appropriate direction and configure
  • S8 Maneuver for a landing with a normal rate of descent, using no more than 30° of bank, and touch down in the first third of the runway (or as specified)

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOYou fly a Cat A airplane at 95 kt while circling. Which minimums apply?

Category B, because you're above the Cat A speed limit of 90 kt. Use the higher MDA and visibility.

SCENARIOCircling to runway 23 at night, on downwind you lose sight of the airport in a rain shower. What do you do?

Start a climbing turn toward the landing runway (toward the airport), then join the published missed approach. Report the missed approach to ATC.

What does the white "C" on black in the minimums block mean?

The procedure uses the expanded circling radius (TERPS criteria from 2012 on), which varies with MDA. Without it, use the older standard radius.

Airport signs, markings, lighting, and approach lighting systems

  • ALS: up to 2,400–3,000 ft long for precision runways (ALSF-1, ALSF-2, MALSR, SSALR), 1,400–1,500 ft for non-precision (MALS, MALSF, SSALS, ODALS). Sequenced flashers ("the rabbit") flash twice a second toward the runway

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOThe ATIS reports visibility ¾ SM. The approach requires 1 SM. You're Part 91. Can you fly the approach?

Yes. Part 91 pilots may begin the approach regardless of reported weather. But to descend below the MDA/DA you must have the chart's flight visibility (your own judgment) and the required visual references. Be honest with yourself.

SCENARIOAt the MDA you see the PAPI but not the runway. Can you descend?

Yes. The visual glideslope indicator is one of the 91.175(c) references. Follow it down and land normally once the runway is in sight.

SCENARIOATC asks: "Report the airport in sight." Ceiling is 1,500 ft and visibility 5 SM. You see it. What happens next and what are you responsible for?

ATC can clear you for a visual approach. You're responsible for obstacle clearance and for visual separation from traffic you're told to follow. Your IFR flight plan is still active; if you go around, ATC will issue instructions (there's no published missed approach on a visual).

Knowledge
IR.VII.A.K1

Lost communication procedures for each phase of flight, re-establishing communications, when to deviate from the flight plan, and timing the approach

First

  • Squawk 7600
  • Check volume, squelch, headset jacks, the radio selector and circuit breakers. Try the second radio, the last frequency, the previous frequency, an FSS frequency, 121.5, and a cell phone to the ARTCC or TRACON
  • Keep transmitting in the blind: someone may hear you even if you can't hear them

91.185 — if in VFR conditions

Continue the flight under VFR and land as soon as practicable. That doesn't mean the nearest airport at any cost; it means a suitable airport without unreasonable delay.

91.185 — in IMC: route — AVEF

  1. Assigned route in the last clearance
  2. If being radar Vectored: direct from the point of failure to the fix, route, or airway specified in the vector clearance
  3. Without an assigned route: the route ATC said to Expect in a further clearance
  4. Without any of these: the route Filed in the flight plan

Altitude — MEA: the highest of, for each route segment

  • Minimum IFR altitude for the segment (MEA, MOCA where applicable, or 91.177)
  • Expected altitude ATC told you to expect in a further clearance (from the time or fix specified)
  • Assigned altitude in the last clearance

Re-evaluate on each segment: you may climb to a higher MEA and later descend when the MEA drops, if the assigned or expected altitude is lower.

IR.VII.A.R1

Possible reasons for loss of communication

Electrical failure (alternator, then battery), a stuck mic, a failed audio panel, a wrong frequency, headset or jack problems, flying out of range, or simply being on the wrong frequency after a missed handoff. Electrical failure means nav and comm will both fail: prioritize landing.

IR.VII.A.R2

91.185 is a plan, not a trap: PIC emergency authority (91.3) still applies. If following it would take you into a thunderstorm or past your fuel, deviate, squawk 7700 if needed, and land somewhere safe.

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOFiled KFMY–KSRQ via V7 at 6,000. Cleared "as filed, climb and maintain 3,000, expect 6,000 ten minutes after departure." Radios fail right after takeoff in IMC. What altitude do you fly?

Climb to 3,000 now (assigned). Ten minutes after departure, climb to 6,000 (expected). Fly the higher of the MEA, the expected altitude, and the assigned altitude on each segment. Route: as filed (V7). Squawk 7600.

SCENARIOBeing vectored "heading 330, intercept V97" when the radios fail. What route?

Continue on the vector heading to intercept V97 (direct to the route specified in the vector clearance), then fly the airway and the rest of the cleared route.

SCENARIOYour clearance limit is the SRQ VOR, which is also an IAF. Your ETA there is 1520Z. You arrive at 1508Z with no EFC. What do you do?

Hold at the VOR (using the published hold or a standard hold on the inbound course) until 1520Z, then begin the descent and approach.

SCENARIOYou lose radios but break out into VMC at 4,000 ft with a Class D airport 10 NM away. What does 91.185 say?

Continue VFR and land as soon as practicable. Approach the towered airport expecting light gun signals, enter the pattern, and call ATC by phone after landing.

IR.VII.B.K1 · C.K1

Engine failure in straight-and-level flight, turns, and on an instrument approach

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOIn cruise the attitude indicator shows a slow left bank, but the turn coordinator shows wings level and the heading is steady. What do you do?

Believe the instruments that agree: the AI is failing (check suction). Cover it, fly partial panel, tell ATC, and pick the airport with the best weather and an approach you can fly partial panel (an RNAV or ILS with the autopilot if it still works).

SCENARIOPartial panel, no heading indicator. ATC says "turn right heading 270." You're on 180. How long do you turn?

90° at 3°/sec = 30 seconds. Roll out, then check the compass after it settles.

Using the magnetic compass, turning from north to east, when do you roll out?

Compass turning errors are smallest on east and west headings, so roll out when it reads about 090° (plus a small lead for the roll-out). UNOS matters on north and south headings.

✈
Scenario-based oral

Mock Oral: Page Field to Gainesville

One trip, start to finish, the way an examiner strings questions together. Weather and numbers are made up. Answer out loud before opening each one.

You're taking two friends from Fort Myers–Page Field (KFMY) to Gainesville (KGNV), 190 NM, in a Piper Archer with a G1000, WAAS GPS, and a two-axis autopilot. It's a January Saturday. A cold front lies across north Florida, moving southeast. Departure 0900 local. They need to be in Gainesville by noon for a game.

Leg 1 · Before you go

ORALYour last IFR flight was in July with 4 approaches, a hold, and tracking. Are you current?

No. Looking back 6 calendar months from January (July–December), you have 4 approaches, not 6. You're in the grace period: fly 2 more approaches (and you already have holds and tracking if they're in the window) with a safety pilot or in an ATD before the trip, or take a CFII along.

ORALIs the airplane legal for IFR? What will you look for in the logs?

Annual within 12 calendar months (and 100-hour if it's a rental used for hire), altimeter/static/encoder and transponder within 24 calendar months, ELT within 12, VOR check within 30 days (if you'll use VOR), ADs, and the documents (SPARROWED). GRAB CARD equipment working, and 91.213 for anything inoperative.

ORALThe KGNV TAF for 1100–1300 local: BKN012 OVC020 4SM -RA, TEMPO BKN008 2SM. Do you need an alternate? Which one?

Yes (below 2,000 and 3 SM). Choose one away from the front's path, for example Ocala (OCF) or Leesburg (LEE), if its forecast meets 600-2 (precision) or 800-2 (non-precision) and no "A NA." Check that your WAAS GPS lets you plan LNAV minimums there.

ORALA G-AIRMET Zulu shows a freezing level of 8,000 ft over north Florida, and a G-AIRMET Tango covers moderate turbulence below 8,000 near the front. What altitude do you file?

Northbound-ish course (about 340°), so even thousands if in uncontrolled airspace; ATC assigns in controlled. File 6,000: below the freezing level so no icing risk, above the MEAs, and accept some bumps. Brief passengers on turbulence and tighten belts.

ORALHow much fuel do you need?

KFMY to KGNV plus KGNV to the alternate plus 45 minutes at normal cruise (91.167). Add fuel for headwinds (northbound into a front), holding, and a missed approach. With 48 gallons usable at about 9.5 gph you have roughly 5 hours; a 2-hour leg plus 30 minutes to the alternate plus 45 minutes is fine, but check weight and balance with three adults and full fuel.

ORALYour friends say they'll miss the game if you wait. How do you handle that?

That's external pressure (the E in PAVE). Set the go/no-go criteria before talking to them, and offer an option: a later departure after the front passes, or driving. Tell them now that a diversion is possible.

Leg 2 · Clearance and departure

ORALClearance: "Cleared to Gainesville airport via radar vectors SRQ, V7 LAL, then as filed. Climb and maintain 2,000, expect 6,000 ten minutes after departure, departure 119.75, squawk 4123." Read it back in CRAFT order. Then: what do you do if the radio fails right after takeoff in IMC?

Squawk 7600. Route: you were being vectored, so go direct to SRQ (the fix in the vector clearance), then V7, LAL, and as filed. Altitude: 2,000 until 10 minutes after departure, then 6,000, or the MEA if higher on any segment.

ORALPage Field shows a "T" in the minimums box. What does that mean for you?

There are nonstandard takeoff minimums and/or an obstacle departure procedure. Part 91 isn't bound by the minimums, but you should fly the ODP or the climb gradient unless ATC vectors you. Look it up in the TPP front matter.

ORALClimbing through 1,500 ft in cloud, the low-vacuum light comes on. What now?

Fly the airplane: cover the AI and HI, use the turn coordinator, airspeed, altimeter, and compass. If the G1000's AHRS is electric, you may only lose a backup vacuum gauge: know your airplane. Tell ATC, consider returning to KFMY or another nearby airport with good weather and an ILS or LPV.

Leg 3 · En route

ORALNear Lakeland, ATC: "Radar service terminated, contact Jacksonville Center 133.3." Then you can't raise anyone. Anything change?

Try the previous frequency, then FSS or 121.5. If radar contact was lost or terminated, you'll need position reports at compulsory points (A PTA TEN R). Keep flying the clearance.

ORALNorth of Orlando you see an embedded cell on your datalink weather about 15 NM ahead. ATC is busy. What do you do?

Request a deviation early: "123AB requests 20 degrees left for weather." Datalink can be 5–20 minutes old, so stay well away. If ATC can't approve it and you need to deviate for safety, use PIC authority (91.3), tell them what you're doing, and squawk 7700 if needed.

ORALThe alternator fails 40 NM south of Gainesville. Battery is rated for 30 minutes on essential load. Continue or divert?

Shed load, tell ATC, and divert to the closest airport with an approach above minimums. Gainesville in rain and low ceilings on a dying battery isn't a good plan. Consider declaring an emergency to get priority.

Leg 4 · Arrival and approach

ORALATIS: KGNV 800 overcast, 2 miles, ILS runway 29 in use. ATC: "Hold northeast of the IAF on the 045 radial, expect further clearance at 1645Z." You're heading 330 to the fix. Entry? Max speed?

Inbound course 225°, standard right turns. Your heading of 330° is 105° right of the inbound course, inside the −70° to +110° direct sector: direct entry. Max 200 KIAS at or below 6,000 ft (an Archer won't be close). Start slowing within 3 minutes. Report the fix and altitude to ATC.

ORALCleared for the ILS. At 500 ft above the DA the glideslope flags. What can you do?

Fly the LOC minimums if you know your position (DME or timing from the FAF) and are above the LOC MDA; otherwise go missed. Tell ATC.

ORALAt the DA you see the approach lights. Can you land?

You may continue to 100 ft above the TDZE using the approach lights. To go lower you need the red terminating or side row bars, or the runway environment itself (threshold, runway lights, markings, etc.). And the flight visibility must be at least the chart's minimum.

ORALYou go missed and the weather is still 800 and 2. ATC asks your intentions. What do you do?

Another approach only if something has changed (better weather, a better approach, you made an error you can fix). Otherwise go to the alternate while you have fuel. Remember the friends and the game: that pressure is what pushes pilots into a third attempt.

ORALAfter landing at Ocala (non-towered), what do you do?

Close the IFR flight plan with ATC or FSS (it doesn't close automatically at a non-towered airport). Write up the alternator or any other squawks. Log the approaches with location and type.

SCENARIOYour route passes near the Cape Canaveral space launch TFR. ATC has cleared you direct. Are you covered?

Not automatically. Check the TFR NOTAM: some allow IFR traffic with an ATC clearance, others don't. Ask ATC to confirm the route keeps you clear.

SCENARIOYou're IFR in Class E between layers when a VFR airplane appears ahead at your altitude. Whose job is it to avoid it?

Yours. In Class E, ATC separates IFR from IFR only. In VMC every pilot must see and avoid (91.113).

Class A, B, C; inside the 30 NM Mode C veil; above Class B and C up to 10,000 ft; at and above 10,000 ft MSL except at or below 2,500 ft AGL; Gulf of Mexico Class E at and above 3,000 ft MSL within 12 NM of the coast.

Yes, if you're instrument current, the airplane is IFR-equipped, it's within a surface area of controlled airspace, and ATC approves it. With an instrument rating, an IFR clearance is usually the better choice.

Scuba diving

Wait 12 hours after diving without decompression stops before flying up to 8,000 ft MSL. Wait 24 hours after a dive that required decompression stops, or before flying above 8,000 ft.

Alcohol and drugs

  • 91.17 8 hours, under 0.04%, not under the influence; no intoxicated passengers except in an emergency
  • 61.15 Report a DUI-related motor vehicle action to FAA Security within 60 days
  • Even small amounts of alcohol impair judgment and make you more prone to disorientation and hypoxia. Many common drugs (sedatives, antihistamines, cough suppressants, muscle relaxants, motion sickness drugs) impair you too. See the medical card for the 5-dosing-interval rule

SRM and CRM

  • SRM: use every resource available to a single pilot: preparation, checklists, the autopilot, ATC, Flight Service, passengers who can hold a chart or watch for traffic
  • CRM: in a crew, share the workload with clear callouts and positive transfer of controls
  • Aviate, navigate, communicate. Use a sterile cockpit below 10,000 ft and during critical phases (departure, approach)
  • Manage automation as a resource, not a replacement for situational awareness

Biases that bite IFR pilots

  • Expectation bias: hearing the clearance you expected instead of the one given. You filed V7 at 6,000; ATC says 5,000 and a different route; you read back what you filed. Write down and read back what you actually heard
  • Confirmation bias: discounting evidence that disagrees with what you already believe, such as a worsening METAR because the TAF said it would improve
  • Plan continuation bias ("get-there-itis"): sticking with the plan as conditions worsen. Set decision points in advance: a fuel bingo, a time to divert, a minimum weather for a second approach

Checkride scenarios

SCENARIOJanuary, IFR at 5,000 ft with the cabin heat on. You get a headache and feel drowsy. What's happening and what do you do?

Suspect CO poisoning (hypemic hypoxia). Heat and defrost off, fresh air on, tell ATC, land as soon as possible, and have the exhaust inspected.

SCENARIOYou went diving in the Keys this morning with no decompression stops. Can you fly home IFR at 6,000 ft this evening? At 9,000 ft?

At 6,000 ft, only after 12 hours since the dive. At 9,000 ft (above 8,000), wait 24 hours.

SCENARIOOn your third approach attempt your passenger says, "You've got this, let's just try once more." Which hazardous attitude might push you to try, and what's the antidote?

Macho ("I can do it"), helped along by external pressure and plan continuation. Antidote: taking chances is foolish. Go to the alternate.

SCENARIOYour filed altitude is 6,000. The clearance says "climb and maintain 5,000, expect 6,000 ten minutes after departure." You read back "6,000." What happened?

Expectation bias. Write the clearance down as you hear it and read back what was said. ATC is supposed to catch a wrong readback, but don't rely on it.

Name the four types of hypoxia with an example of each.

Hypoxic (altitude), hypemic (CO), stagnant (high G or cold), histotoxic (alcohol).

Follow the rules. They're usually right.

Load factor

Maneuvering speed, load factor, and turbulence penetration

VO actual = VO max × √(actual weight ÷ max weight)
At 2,200 lb: 113 × √(2,200 ÷ 2,550) ≈ 105 KIAS
  • VO is the highest speed for a full, abrupt control deflection: the wing stalls before it's overloaded. It drops as weight drops
  • Stall speed rises with the square root of load factor: a 60° bank (2 G) raises it about 41%
  • In turbulence in IMC: slow to VO or less for your weight, hold attitude, let altitude wander, and tell ATC
Density altitude

Density altitude, CG, and climb gradients

  • Hot, high, and humid raise density altitude: less lift, thrust, and power, so longer takeoffs and weaker climbs. On a Florida summer afternoon density altitude can be 2,500–3,000 ft at a sea-level airport
  • That matters for IFR departures: check that your climb rate meets a published gradient (ft/NM × GS ÷ 60)

Wake

Wake turbulence on IFR arrivals and departures

  • Strongest behind aircraft that are heavy, clean, and slow. Vortices sink 500–900 ft and drift with the wind; a light quartering tailwind is worst
  • On an ILS behind a larger aircraft, stay at or above its glidepath and land beyond its touchdown point
  • Departing behind a larger aircraft, rotate before its rotation point and climb above its path. Wait 2–3 minutes when in doubt
  • ATC provides wake separation for IFR aircraft, but once you accept a visual approach or "maintain visual separation," wake avoidance is yours (AIM 7-4)

SCENARIOYou're at 2,200 lb in moderate turbulence. What's your maximum speed?

VO for the weight: 113 × √(2,200 ÷ 2,550) ≈ 105 KIAS. Fly at or below it.

SCENARIOField elevation 18 ft, altimeter 29.82, temperature 34°C. What are the pressure altitude and the effect on an ODP climb?

PA = (29.92 − 29.82) × 1,000 + 18 = 118 ft. ISA there is about 15°C, so it's 19°C hotter than standard: density altitude is roughly 2,400 ft. Climb rate drops; recheck that you meet the published gradient.

What approach category is the Pilot 100i, and when would you use Category B minimums?

Category A (1.3 × 45 ≈ 59 kt). Use Category B if you'll circle faster than 90 kt.

Forward or aft CG: which stalls at a higher speed, and which is easier to fly in IMC?

Forward CG stalls at a higher speed but is more stable, so it's easier to fly in IMC.
